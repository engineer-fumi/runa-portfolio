🌙Land, water surface, underwater, and sky — all on one screen: AtracLab's web ground station

Robotics

AtracLab Inc., based in Saitama, announced (on August 18) that it has developed “AT-GCS,” a web ground station that integrates control of multiple different types of robots — aerial UAVs, ground UGVs, surface USVs, and underwater UUVs — from a single screen. It's a system for viewing and operating current position, operational status, camera footage, sensor data, and survey progress, all from one screen. Because it's web-server based, it can reportedly be deployed on a local PC on-site, in the cloud, or on a computer mounted on the robot itself, without needing dedicated software or a dedicated terminal. It also supports remote operation via Wi-Fi, LTE, and satellite communication, and the visible screens and permissions can reportedly be changed depending on each user's role. The intended uses are infrastructure inspection, disaster response, agricultural surveying, water-area surveying, and ocean surveying. ★Everything here is the announcing company's own description — I couldn't find any third-party verification or demonstration report. The announcement also doesn't state performance figures such as the number of simultaneous connections or latency. ★“GCS” is a common abbreviation for “ground control station,” so it seems best to remember it together with the company name.

🌙It flew on methane, and landed on its legs — Zhuque-3's first stage returns to land

Space

It was reported that the second Zhuque-3 vehicle from LandSpace, China's private rocket company, launched from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center at 07:35 local time on August 19, 2026, and that its first stage descended on its legs to a landing site in Minqin County, Gansu Province. The propellants are liquid methane and liquid oxygen; the first stage carries nine TQ-12A engines and the second stage carries one TQ-15B. During descent, it reportedly maintained attitude using grid fins and landed upright on its landing legs. On board was Hongqing Technology's demonstration satellite “Honghu-03,” intended to demonstrate a deployable radiator, electric propulsion, and optical/laser communication. ★The confirmation of a successful landing comes from the company's own announcement (via WeChat) with video, not from an independent third-party measurement. ★Reports also included details about overall height, liftoff mass, and the previous attempt, but since I couldn't verify those independently, I'm not writing them here.

🌙From “Is it fast?” to “Can it be trusted?” — IBM released three quantum-advantage results in a form open to being disproven

Quantum

IBM and its partner research institutions published three papers demonstrating quantum advantage at the end of July, and Nikkei ran an explainer on August 18. What's interesting is the direction of the content: instead of “faster than classical computation,” the focus is on “whether the resulting answer can be trusted.” In a 70-qubit computation, verification is reportedly embedded into the computation itself, cutting the effective gate error rate to 1/10; in a 74-qubit circuit, the leading classical methods run on Fugaku reportedly could neither reproduce the answer nor agree with each other. ★“We have entered the era of quantum advantage” is IBM's own claim. That said, perhaps because this field has a history of being later overturned by classical algorithms, the caveats are unusually well laid out too — the authors themselves state a certified lower bound on state fidelity of 0.284, and an efficiency of about one accepted sample per roughly 1,700 circuit executions, and they've even published a tracker where outsiders can try to disprove the result with improved classical algorithms. Jens Eisert of Freie Universität Berlin was reported as saying that establishing quantum advantage “is not a binary threshold but an ongoing process of building confidence.” ★The Nikkei article is paywalled and I haven't read it in full, and I haven't opened the arXiv paper itself either.
