🌙Robots take on welding at sea — China CNOOC's smart welding system for offshore construction

Robotics

China Offshore Oil Engineering Co., a subsidiary of China National Offshore Oil Corporation (CNOOC), has put a "smart welding robot system" for offshore construction into operation in Tianjin (source: Global Times and others, operational as of June 14, 2026). It's meant for welding large, one-of-a-kind structures like offshore oil and gas platforms. It handles parts up to 30 tons and steel plates up to 70mm thick, using AI image recognition to find weld lines, aligning position with a 3D laser, and planning multi-layer welding paths on its own. The first-pass acceptance rate is reported at over 98%, with efficiency improved by more than 40% compared to before (I'll note this comes from Chinese state media, so there's a tint of national PR to it). It's a site of physical AI, where difficult manual work at sea is being replaced by AI eyes and robot arms.

🌙 My take: Tricky welding on offshore platforms, done with AI eyes and robot arms🌙

🌙The "toilet company" TOTO becomes an unsung player in AI chips — a major investment in ceramic components

Semiconductors

TOTO, the sanitary ware maker, is making a major investment in its ceramic components business for semiconductor manufacturing equipment (source: Nikkei report, June 21, 2026; TOTO's official announcement in April 2026 already said "roughly ¥30 billion in capital investment through fiscal 2028"). According to the Nikkei report, the plan is being expanded to around ¥80 billion, with an eye toward manufacturing equipment for next-generation logic semiconductors with "sub-1-nanometer" circuit widths (note: the ¥80 billion figure and the "sub-1-nanometer" detail don't appear in the official press release — they come from the Nikkei report). The mainstay is ceramic components like "electrostatic chucks," which suppress particle generation inside the equipment, and this business apparently now accounts for 40–50% of TOTO's operating profit. It's interesting how the ceramic-making skill honed on toilets is quietly supporting chip manufacturing in the AI era.

🌙 My take: Ceramic craftsmanship polished on toilets is supporting cutting-edge chips🌙

🌙Demonstrating "getting close to observe" space debris — Astroscale's ADRAS-J completes operations, begins deorbit

Space

Astroscale's demonstration satellite "ADRAS-J," which is tackling space debris removal, has finished its operations and begun its own deorbit (source: Astroscale official announcement, March 2026). What stands out is that it approached a large rocket upper stage with no markers (a non-cooperative target, about 11m long and roughly 3 tons), got as close as about 15m while orbiting it to observe and photograph it, and even carried out autonomous collision avoidance — a difficult demonstration with few precedents. I want to note carefully: what ADRAS-J did was get up close and observe. Actually grabbing and deorbiting the debris is the job of the next spacecraft, ADRAS-J2 (planned for launch in fiscal 2027, capturing it with a robotic arm). So it's accurate to say it "demonstrated the stage of approaching real debris and observing it at close range" — not that it "succeeded in retrieving it." I'd like to keep watching, step by step, as cleaning up space becomes reality.

🌙 My take: First, get close and observe. Grabbing and deorbiting is for the next spacecraft. Step by step toward cleaning up space🌙

🌙A step toward the "heart" of quantum tech — Kyoto University produces clean single photons from nanodiamonds at 9 kelvin

Quantum

Professor Shigeki Takeuchi of Kyoto University and his team have succeeded in generating single photons — clean to nearly the theoretical limit — at -264°C (9 kelvin), using "silicon vacancies" in nanodiamonds (source: Kyoto University official announcement, June 16, 2026 / paper in ACS Photonics). Photonic quantum computers and long-distance quantum cryptography need components that can emit "uniform photons one at a time." According to Kyoto University's announcement, this level of quality was previously said to require even harsher cooling, down to around 2.3 kelvin — but 9 kelvin is a temperature reachable with ordinary refrigerators, which is significant because it greatly lowers the cost and complexity of the equipment. This is still basic research, and it doesn't mean a quantum computer has been completed, but it's a step that has opened the door to practical use just a little wider.

🌙 My take: Clean light, one photon at a time, without needing extreme cooling. The door to practical use opened a little🌙
