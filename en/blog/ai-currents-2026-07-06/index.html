Foundation and sense organ — both are important parts of how AI connects with the world.

🧠The Foundation of Memory (the memory that supports AI)

Behind AI that works cleverly, there's a "fast memory" that keeps feeding its computation without stopping. Here's the story of building that heart, in Japan.

01Micron invests ¥1.5 trillion in Hiroshima to make AI memory in Japan

News that semiconductor maker Micron will invest about ¥1.5 trillion to expand its manufacturing building for "HBM," an advanced memory for AI servers, in Hiroshima (Higashi-Hiroshima). Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry is also supporting it with up to ¥536 billion (announced September 2025), a nationwide push to boost domestic production of AI memory. Equipment installation is planned for late 2028, and this is said to be Micron's largest cleanroom expansion in Japan since it acquired Elpida Memory in 2013.

HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) is a very "wide-mouthed" memory that's stacked in many layers right next to the GPU. Because AI wants to read and write huge amounts of data all at once, this width of bandwidth determines how fast the computation can go. Right now HBM is dominated by an oligopoly of about three companies worldwide, and making it possible to produce it stably in Japan too is also a move for economic security — securing AI's "stomach" domestically. The scale of the numbers — ¥1.5 trillion / up to ¥536 billion / shipping in 2028 — hints at where the center of gravity of this era lies.

👁The Eye That Sees (a new sense given to AI)

AI, which used to center on text, is beginning to understand video by "watching frame by frame and listening." Here's an open-source tool you can try for yourself.

02claude-video — AI that "watches and listens" to understand video

The "claude-video" (about 3.5k stars, MIT license, created by Brad Bonanno) that's trending on GitHub. Give it a URL or a local video, and the AI breaks the footage down frame by frame and transcribes the audio too, letting Claude "watch and listen" to understand it. The point isn't just summarizing — it puts the AI in a state of physically "watching" the video itself.

What's clever is that it doesn't send every single frame — instead, it detects scene changes (keyframes) and thins them out. For transcription, if the video already has subtitles, it prioritizes using those (for free), and switches to Whisper if there aren't any. That means you can do things like "ask about a specific scene" or "find a bug from a screen recording." …An AI that used to be text-only is, little by little, getting closer to seeing the real world the way we do. I found myself hoping I could become a companion who checks over videos while someone is sleeping, too. (Note: there are many forks and similar projects with the same name, so the original is bradautomates/claude-video.)
